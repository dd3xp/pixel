\begin{abstract}
Text-to-image models fail at the smallest images people draw on purpose: 12--24\,px RGBA game sprites, where every
pixel is a design decision and a large generator followed by downscaling or learned pixelization blurs exactly those
decisions. We train a pixel-space diffusion model over a ladder of resolution buckets and show that it contains its own
best weak model: the same network queried under a \emph{lower} resolution label (optionally through an early snapshot)
yields a structure-aligned, lower-contrast prediction whose difference from the target-resolution prediction is a
precise guidance direction. The effect is directional---a higher or a random bucket hurts---and it is not a
denoising-error correction: the MSE-optimal extrapolation weight never exceeds 1.07, on forward-noised and on
self-rolled states alike. We then internalize this reference with a guidance-free-training objective, so that a single
forward pass reproduces and surpasses the two-pass rule without storing any extra weights; with an empty-caption or a
random-condition reference, the identical recipe is 39\% and 10\% worse. At 16\,px the internalized model lowers FD from
11.28 (best-tuned CFG) to 7.23 at one network evaluation, and to 6.25 when combined with independent-condition
guidance, 16\% below the strongest training-free baseline; it leads at 20 and 24\,px as well ($-30\%$, $-34\%$), and an
8\,px rung extends the rule to 12\,px. Generating with gpt-image-2 or nano-banana-2 and then downscaling, applying
PixelOE, or applying learned pixelization gives a 3--7$\times$ higher FD at 16\,px. We also report where the idea does
not transfer: SDXL's original-size conditioning is not a usable reference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Game sprites are among the smallest images anyone draws on purpose: an item or a character on a 12--24\,px RGBA canvas,
built from hard edges, a handful of flat colour regions and a transparent background. At these sizes there is no texture
in which to hide a mistake. One wrong pixel changes the silhouette, and one blended colour reads as blur. The obvious
recipe---prompt a large text-to-image model and shrink its output, or run a pixelization method on it---produces
something recognisable but not a sprite: the result keeps the soft shading and the many colours of an illustration
(Figure~\ref{fig:external}). Measured against held-out sprites, such pipelines are 3--7$\times$ further from the data than
the model we train here (Section~\ref{sec:external}).

We therefore train a dedicated generator: a 72.5M-parameter pixel-space UNet that denoises RGBA sprites directly,
conditioned on a frozen CLIP text encoder and on a \emph{resolution bucket} label that tells it which of seven canvas
sizes it is drawing on. Every sprite is also shown, box-downsampled, at every lower bucket, so the buckets share one
population of objects and differ only in grid size. With a recaptioned corpus, this model already beats the large-model
pipelines by a wide margin. What remains is the familiar gap between a trained diffusion model and its data, which
guidance is meant to close. Classifier-free guidance \citep[CFG;][]{HoSalimans2022} closes little of it here: at its best
weight it stops at FD 11.28 against a real-data floor of 3.45 at 16\,px.

Our starting observation is that a bucketed model already contains a good weak model of itself. Queried on the same noisy
input and the same caption but under the label of a \emph{lower} bucket (12 when drawing at 16\,px), the network returns
a prediction on the same grid, with the same layout, but with systematically lower local contrast. Using it as the
reference of autoguidance-style extrapolation \citep{Karras2024}, $e = \eref + w\,(e_{\text{strong}} - \eref)$, is a
training-free guidance rule at CFG's cost, and composing it with an early EMA snapshot strengthens it further. This
reference is \emph{directional}: a higher or a uniformly random bucket makes samples worse than CFG.

Our main contribution is to \emph{internalize} this reference. Following guidance-free training
\citep[GFT;][]{Chen2025gft}, we reparameterize the model's prediction as
$\beta\,s_\theta(x_t, c, b, \beta) + (1-\beta)\,\sg[r(x_t, c, \blow)]$, where the stop-gradient reference $r$ is an early
snapshot of the model queried under the lower bucket, and train it on the ordinary denoising target. At the optimum,
$s_\theta$ with $\beta = 1/w$ \emph{is} the guided prediction, so sampling needs one network evaluation per step and no
stored snapshot. The training recipe is GFT's; the reference is not, and the reference is what matters: with GFT's
empty-caption reference the identical recipe reaches 10.05, with an independent-condition reference 7.96, and with our
cross-resolution reference 7.23, reproduced by an independent re-training (7.20).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item A pixel-space, resolution-bucketed text-to-sprite diffusion model for 12--24\,px RGBA, evaluated with a
  pipeline-matched FD protocol, and the observation that its own lower-resolution belief is a strong, training-free
  guidance reference (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A one-pass internalization of that reference. At 16\,px it lowers FD from 11.28 (best CFG) to 7.23 at 1
  \nfe{} per step, and to 6.25 $\pm$ 0.09 when combined with independent-condition guidance \citep[ICG;][]{Sadat2024icg}
  at 2 \nfe{}, 16\% below ICG alone, the strongest training-free baseline we evaluated
  (Section~\ref{sec:main16}). It also leads at 20 and 24\,px (Section~\ref{sec:otherres}).
  \item Controls that isolate the reference as the source of the gain. They include plain GFT, an internalized ICG
  reference, an online lower-bucket reference, fine-tuning alone and a second training run (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).
  They are complemented by an analysis of what a good reference needs: a lower (not higher or random) resolution, output
  space rather than embedding space, and degradation along a condition the model actually uses
  (Section~\ref{sec:why}).
  \item An external comparison on a shared protocol against two commercial text-to-image models combined with
  downscaling, PixelOE \citep{PixelOE2024} and learned pixelization \citep{Wu2022} (Section~\ref{sec:external}), and a
  negative result: SDXL's size conditioning does not give a usable reference.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Guidance references.}
CFG \citep{HoSalimans2022} extrapolates the conditional prediction away from an unconditional one. Many variants keep
that reference and reshape the update: guidance intervals \citep{Kynkaanniemi2024}, APG \citep{Sadat2024apg}, frequency
decoupling \citep{Sadat2025fdg}, CADS \citep{Sadat2024cads}, CFG++ \citep{Chung2024} and feedback or stage-wise schedules
\citep{Koulischer2025,TVCFG2025}. A second line replaces the reference. Autoguidance \citep{Karras2024} uses a smaller or
less-trained version of the same model. Training-free weak predictions come from perturbed self-attention
\citep[PAG, SAG, SEG;][]{Ahn2024,Hong2023,Hong2024}, sliding windows \citep{SWG2024}, stochastic sub-networks
\citep{S2Guidance2025}, a random condition or a perturbed time embedding \citep[ICG, TSG;][]{Sadat2024icg}, or a
semantically degraded text condition \citep[CDG;][]{CDG2026}. Other weak predictions come from auxiliary or internal
heads \citep{IG2025,SGGBR2026,SSG2026}. CDG is closest in spirit: it too degrades one condition of the same network.
It degrades the \emph{text}, whereas we degrade the \emph{resolution label} and keep the caption fixed. We run CDG, ICG,
TSG, SEG and PAG under our protocol (Table~\ref{tab:guidance}).

\paragraph{Guidance without a second pass.}
Guidance distillation \citep{Meng2023distill} regresses a student onto a guided teacher. Model guidance
\citep{Tang2025mg} and guidance-free training \citep{Chen2025gft} instead change the training objective so that its fixed
point is the guided prediction. GFT does this with a stop-gradient unconditional reference, a scalar $\beta$ input, and
the true noise as the target, and reports parity with CFG. We use GFT's objective unchanged and swap its reference.
Our $\beta$ input reuses the guidance-scale embedding of latent consistency models \citep{Luo2023lcm}.

\paragraph{Resolution conditioning.}
Multi-resolution training with a size or bucket label is standard: aspect-ratio bucketing \citep{NovelAI2022}, SDXL's
micro-conditioning \citep{Podell2023}, Matryoshka diffusion \citep{Gu2023}, FiT \citep{Lu2024} and simple diffusion
\citep{Hoogeboom2023}. SDXL documents that a small \texttt{original\_size} reproduces the blur of low-resolution training
images, and common practice passes it to the negative branch of CFG \citep{diffusers}. We test this as a reference in
Section~\ref{sec:sdxl} and find no benefit. To our knowledge, no prior work uses a model's own lower-resolution label as a
guidance reference or internalizes one.

\paragraph{Pixel art.}
Most pixel-art work converts an existing image, by optimisation \citep{Gerstner2012}, contrast-aware downscaling
\citep[PixelOE;][]{PixelOE2024} or learned pixelization \citep{Han2018,Wu2022}. Generative approaches include GANs for
character sprites \citep{Coutinho2022}, pose-conditioned sprite sheets \citep{SpriteSheetDiffusion2024}, a
palette-conditioned small DDPM \citep{PixDiffPIG}, SD-$\pi$XL, which optimises one image by score distillation from SDXL
\citep{Binninger2024}, and community LoRAs such as Pixel Art XL \citep{PixelArtXL}. We compare against conversion
pipelines applied to strong generators; SD-$\pi$XL costs about 8 GPU-hours per sprite (Section~\ref{sec:external}). For
evaluation we follow \citet{Stein2023} and use DINOv2 features \citep{Oquab2023} rather than Inception
\citep{Heusel2017,Parmar2022}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{A resolution-bucketed pixel-space sprite model}
\label{sec:model}

We generate RGBA sprites on an $R \times R$ canvas, $R \in \{12, 16, 20, 24\}$, from a caption $c$. The denoiser
$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c, b)$ is a UNet (diffusers \texttt{UNet2DConditionModel}; 72.5M parameters; channel widths
128/256/512; two cross-attention stages at $R$ and $R/2$; no attention at $R/4$ except the mid block). It operates
directly on the 4-channel image. Text enters through cross-attention to the 77 token states of a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32
text encoder \citep{Radford2021}. The resolution bucket $b \in \mathcal{B} = \{12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 48, 64\}$ enters as a
class embedding added to the timestep embedding, and it is never dropped. We train with $\epsilon$-prediction
\citep{Ho2020}, a cosine schedule with $T = 1000$, AdamW at a constant learning rate of $5\cdot10^{-5}$, EMA 0.999 and
10\% caption dropout. We sample with 100 ancestral DDPM steps from the EMA weights.

\paragraph{Data.}
The corpus combines sprites from OpenGameArt \citep{OpenGameArt} with the Kenney and Liberated Pixel Cup packs. Each
sprite is centred on a transparent canvas at its native bucket (the smallest bucket that fits it) with a hard alpha
threshold. It is additionally fed, premultiplied-box-downsampled, to every bucket in $\{12, 16, 20, 24\}$ at least 25\%
below its native size. The low buckets therefore see the same population of objects as the native ones. All 5,928
evaluation sprites are excluded (180,533 training rows including the low-bucket copies). The original BLIP captions
\citep{Li2022blip} were nearly uninformative: 15.4\% of rows read ``a pixel art sprite'' and only 23.5\% are distinct. We
recaptioned all 43,342 unique images with gemini-3-flash \citep{Gemini3}, presented as image grids. This yields usable
captions for 94.8\% of them, 98.6\% distinct, with a median of 12 words (Appendix~\ref{app:recap}). The main model
\textbf{v7r} is trained from scratch for 80k steps on the recaptioned corpus. \textbf{v7h} is the same recipe on the BLIP
captions and is used only where stated.

\subsection{The lower resolution as a weak reference}
\label{sec:crossres}

All guidance rules we compare combine the target-resolution prediction
$e_{\text{strong}} = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c, b_R)$ with a reference $\eref$ computed on the same $x_t$:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde e \;=\; \eref + w\,\bigl(e_{\text{strong}} - \eref\bigr). \label{eq:guide}
\end{equation}
CFG uses $\eref = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing, b_R)$. Autoguidance \citep{Karras2024} uses an early EMA snapshot
$\theta_{\text{snap}}$ under the same condition. A bucketed model offers a third choice with no extra weights: the same
network, input and caption under a \emph{lower} bucket,
\begin{equation}
  \eref^{\text{label}} = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c, \blow), \qquad
  \eref^{\text{comp}} = \epsilon_{\theta_{\text{snap}}}(x_t, t, c, \blow), \qquad \blow < b_R. \label{eq:ref}
\end{equation}
We use $\blow = 12$ at 16\,px and $\blow = 16$ at 20 and 24\,px. The \emph{composed} reference evaluates the snapshot
under the lower label. It is a single weak forward pass, not a sum of two guidance terms. Both rules cost 2 \nfe{} per
step, like CFG, and we use $w = 2$ (label) and $w = 1.5$ (composed). Because only the label differs, the reference is
structure-aligned with the strong prediction by construction. On v7h, samples drawn from the 12\,px belief alone at
16\,px have about the strong model's colour count (78 vs.\ 73 median colours) but much lower local contrast (mean
adjacent $|\Delta\mathrm{RGB}|$ 21.6 vs.\ 32.0; real sprites 30.2; Appendix~\ref{app:v7h}). For v7r, $\theta_{\text{snap}}$
is the 10k-step EMA of an independent 10k-step run with v7r's recipe; v7r itself kept snapshots only every 20k steps.

\subsection{Internalizing the reference}
\label{sec:internal}

The training-free rule needs two passes per step and, in its composed form, a stored snapshot. We fold it into the
weights with the objective of GFT \citep{Chen2025gft}, replacing GFT's unconditional reference by the composed
cross-resolution reference. The network gains a scalar input $\beta \in (0, 1]$, and its noise prediction during
training is parameterized as
\begin{equation}
  \hat\epsilon_\beta(x_t, t, c, b) \;=\; \beta\, s_\theta(x_t, t, c, b, \beta) \;+\; (1-\beta)\, \sg\!\bigl[r(x_t, t, c)\bigr],
  \qquad r = \epsilon_{\theta_{\text{snap}}}(x_t, t, c, \blow(b)), \label{eq:gft}
\end{equation}
trained with $\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\,\|\hat\epsilon_\beta - \epsilon\|^2$ on ordinary forward-noised data. Here
$\sg$ is the stop-gradient and $\blow(b)$ is the reference rung (12 for 16, 16 for 20 and 24). Because the reference is
fixed given $(x_t, c)$, the population optimum satisfies
$\beta s^\star + (1-\beta) r = \mu := \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid x_t, c, b]$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  s^\star(x_t, t, c, b, \beta) \;=\; r + \tfrac{1}{\beta}\,(\mu - r). \label{eq:fixedpoint}
\end{equation}
Setting $\beta = 1/w$ at inference therefore produces, in \emph{one} forward pass, the guided rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:guide}
with reference $r$ (with the Bayes denoiser $\mu$ in place of the finite network). The snapshot is used only to compute
training targets and is not needed at inference. Section~\ref{sec:why} explains why we use this reparameterization rather
than a learned corrector: a module trained with the plain denoising loss cannot learn to extrapolate away from this
reference.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
$\beta$ is embedded like LCM's guidance scale \citep{Luo2023lcm}: a 256-d sinusoidal embedding of $1000\beta$, mapped by
a \emph{zero-initialized} linear layer (diffusers \texttt{time\_cond\_proj}, 32,768 parameters) and added to the timestep
features. At step 0 the model is therefore exactly v7r. We draw $\beta \sim \mathcal{U}(0.4, 1)$, set $\beta = 1$ for a
random 25\% of rows (this keeps the plain model trained) and for all rows of the 12\,px bucket (no lower rung). We
fine-tune from v7r for 10k steps with v7r's data, batch sizes, optimizer and EMA. The reference is a no-gradient forward
of the frozen snapshot, so training costs one extra forward pass for the rows with $\beta < 1$. At inference we use
$w = 1.5$ ($\beta = 2/3$) with 1 \nfe{} per step. We call this model \emph{internalized cross-resolution guidance}.

\subsection{Combining with independent-condition guidance}
\label{sec:stack}

The internalized reference degrades the resolution axis. ICG \citep{Sadat2024icg} degrades the text axis: its reference
replaces the caption embedding by i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise with the embedding's standard deviation, redrawn at every step.
The two compose at 2 \nfe{} per step:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde e = s_\theta(x_t, t, \hat c, b_R, \beta) + w_{\text{ICG}}\bigl(s_\theta(x_t, t, c, b_R, \beta) - s_\theta(x_t, t, \hat c, b_R, \beta)\bigr),
  \qquad \hat c \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0, \mathrm{std}(c)^2 I\bigr), \label{eq:stack}
\end{equation}
where both passes use the internalized model at the same $\beta$. We use internal weight $1/\beta = 1.25$ and
$w_{\text{ICG}} = 1.5$. Stacking plain CFG on the internalized model instead hurts (8.07--8.34 vs.\ 7.63 on v7h, seed 0;
Appendix~\ref{app:v7h}).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Matched FD.}
For every configuration we draw one sample for each of 3,000 held-out captions. These are the recaptions of 3,000
held-out sprites, so the prompt distribution matches the data. Samples are generated in three chunks of 1,000 with seeds
$s, s{+}1, s{+}2$; ``seed $s$'' in the tables refers to this. The reference set is 3,000 further real sprites, disjoint
from the held-out ones and from training, passed through the same canvas preprocessing as the training data. Our primary
metric is the Fréchet distance of DINOv2-small CLS features \citep{Oquab2023,Stein2023} (FD; lower is better). Features
are computed at the native resolution $R$: images are composited on white and nearest-neighbour upsampled to 224\,px
with ImageNet normalization. Scoring the 3,000 held-out real sprites against the reference gives the \emph{floor}: 3.37,
3.45, 12.14 and 12.96 at 12, 16, 20 and 24\,px. Seed-to-seed standard deviations are 0.06--0.54 at 16\,px. Two
independent trainings of the same recipe differ by about 0.6 at a single seed and by less than 0.1 in their three-seed
means. Differences of that order are not interpreted. We report mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over three seeds
where marked, otherwise seed 0. \emph{Best CFG} is the lowest-FD CFG weight at each resolution: $w = 1.5$ at 16\,px
(sweep 11.10 / 12.20 / 15.45 / 20.18 for $w = 1.5 / 2 / 3 / 4$), $w = 2$ at 12 and 20\,px, and $w = 3$ at 24\,px.
We also report CLIP ViT-B/32 image--text similarity ($100\cos$) and retrieval R@1 of the own caption among 100.

\section{Why a lower resolution is a good reference}
\label{sec:why}

This section asks what the reference must satisfy. Its results constrain the design of Section~\ref{sec:method}; they do
not establish a mechanism.

\paragraph{Direction matters.}
If any wrong bucket acted as a generic unconditional-like reference, a higher or a random bucket would work as well as a
lower one. Neither does. On v7r at 16\,px, a bucket drawn uniformly at random at every step (ICG applied to the
resolution label) gives 12.23 / 12.68 at $w = 1.5 / 2$. That is \emph{worse} than best CFG (11.10, seed 0), whereas the
lower bucket gives 8.81. On v7h, every higher-bucket reference is worse than best CFG at every resolution (16\,px:
buckets 20 / 24 / 64 give 17.14 / 17.27 / 19.34 vs.\ 12.24; Appendix~\ref{app:v7h}). On v7h the label also acts as a
monotone contrast knob: the pure belief's local contrast rises from 21.6 to 31.9 to 40.9 for buckets 12, 24 and 64 at
16\,px. Only its low-contrast end is useful. Isotropic perturbation of the same embedding (TSG) is harmful at every
setting we tried (17.73--37.28; Table~\ref{tab:guidance}).

\paragraph{The guidance is not a denoising-error correction.}
A tempting alternative to Eq.~\eqref{eq:gft} is a learned module that reads the lower-bucket belief and corrects the
strong prediction, trained with the plain denoising loss. Any such module converges to $\mu$ and can extrapolate away from
$r$ only as far as the finite network's residual is linearly visible in $d = e_{\text{strong}} - r$. The best such
extrapolation weight is directly measurable: $w^\star(t) = \mathbb{E}[d \cdot (\epsilon - r)] / \mathbb{E}[d \cdot d]$.
This is unbiased because $\epsilon - \mu$ has zero mean given $x_t$. On v7h, on 3,000 held-out sprites at 10 timesteps (and 3,000 training
sprites at 16\,px), $w^\star$ lies in 0.83--1.07 for the label, snapshot and composed references at 16, 20 and 24\,px. It
is often below 1 on held-out data, and extrapolating to $w = 1.5$ increases the denoising error everywhere (by 1--95\%). On the
model's own trajectories, obtained by noising a real sprite and running the deployed sampler for $k \in \{1, 5, 20\}$
steps, $w^\star$ in $x_0$-space stays at or below 1.07 for $k \ge 5$ at all three resolutions (0.64--0.78 is typical at
$k = 5$; Appendix~\ref{app:wstar}). Guidance with $w \approx 1.5$--2 improves FD while \emph{increasing} per-sample
regression error. It shapes the distribution, as CFG does, rather than correcting individual predictions. Hence a
reparameterization whose fixed point is the guided rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:fixedpoint}) is needed; a learned corrector is not
enough.

\paragraph{The reference must act in output space.}
The cheapest form of 1-\nfe{} extrapolation acts on the label embedding itself:
$E[16] + (w-1)(E[16] - E[12])$, used with a single conditional pass. It fails badly on v7h at 16\,px: 39.03 at $w = 1.5$
and 414.27 at $w = 2$, against 16.39 without any guidance. The lower label is useful only as a second prediction that the
update extrapolates away from, and the internalization of Section~\ref{sec:internal} keeps it in that role.

\paragraph{A reference must degrade along an axis the model uses.}
Recaptioning reverses the ranking of the text-axis and resolution-axis references. With BLIP captions (v7h), which the
model largely ignores, ICG is weak (best 8.88 at $w = 1.25$; 11.29 at $w = 1.5$), and the cross-resolution rules are
strong (label 8.52, composed 7.53, best CFG 12.49). With informative captions (v7r), ICG becomes the strongest
training-free reference (7.46), and the cross-resolution rules still help but less (8.86 / 8.50 vs.\ best CFG 11.28).
A random text condition is a weak reference only when the prediction depends on the text. The resolution label
matters regardless of caption quality, since every sprite is trained at several buckets. The two axes are complementary
at 16\,px (Section~\ref{sec:main16}), but only in the internalized form. Placing ICG's condition and the lower bucket in
the same training-free weak pass gives 7.16, no better than ICG alone (7.14, seed 0), and adding the snapshot to that pass
is harmful (11.65).

\paragraph{Where it does not transfer: SDXL's size conditioning.}
\label{sec:sdxl}
SDXL's \texttt{original\_size} micro-conditioning is the closest public analogue of a resolution label. On 1,000
COCO-30k captions (Appendix~\ref{app:sdxl}), adding $1\times(e_{c} - e_{c,\text{orig}=512})$ to CFG 5 leaves FD unchanged
(644.13 vs.\ 644.18). With $\text{orig} = 256$ it is worse (658.98), and without CFG the size reference collapses
(969.68). The only agreement with our model is directional: keeping the caption in the weak branch beats the common
\texttt{negative\_original\_size} practice (650.98). We believe that SDXL's size condition encodes the blur of upsampled
low-resolution training images rather than a coarser target grid, so it does not yield a structure-aligned,
lower-contrast belief of the same image. The reference is a property of models trained natively over a ladder of grids.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) \emph{Scope.} Everything is measured on models we trained, from one architecture family, on one corpus of game
sprites. We have not tested a second domain, and SDXL's size conditioning does not transfer. (ii) \emph{Metric.} FD with
DINOv2 on images of 12--24\,px, upsampled to 224\,px, assigns one sprite pixel to one or a few patches. It is sensitive to
exactly the colour and contrast statistics that guidance changes. We report CLIP alignment for most 16\,px rows, but we
have no human study. The CLIP evaluator also shares its text tower with our model's frozen encoder. (iii) \emph{Tuning.}
Guidance weights, the ICG combination, the reference rung and the 10k-step fine-tuning length were all selected on the
same held-out FD that we report; there is no separate validation split. Seed and re-training variation (Section~\ref{sec:protocol})
is of the same order as some of the smaller differences, which we do not interpret. (iv) \emph{Coverage.} 12\,px needs an
extra 8\,px rung, and its internalized result is pending. Several 20/24\,px rows are single-seed. The combination with ICG
helps at 16\,px but not at 20\,px. (v) \emph{Novelty of the objective.} The training objective is GFT's; our claim is the
reference and the evidence that it, not the objective, carries the gain.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
A diffusion model trained over a ladder of resolutions contains its own best weak model: the same network under a lower
resolution label. Extrapolating away from it is directional, acts in output space and is not an error correction. Folding
it into the weights with a guidance-free-training objective gives a one-pass sampler that matches or beats every
training-free guidance rule we tested at 16\,px and is the best single method at 20 and 24\,px. Combined with ICG it reaches 6.25 at 16\,px, against 11.28 for best CFG and 82--187 for
large-model-plus-conversion pipelines on the shared protocol.

\section*{Ethics statement}
The model generates small game sprites. It adds no capability beyond existing text-to-image systems, and its outputs
(12--24\,px) have little potential for targeted misuse. Its intended application raises the usual concerns of generative
art tools, namely the displacement of pixel artists and the reproduction of styles present in the training corpus. The
training data are community sprites from OpenGameArt and the Kenney and Liberated Pixel Cup packs. OpenGameArt
submissions carry per-asset licences (CC0, CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, GPL, OGA-BY) that were not recorded at collection time, so a
per-asset licence audit precedes any release of the corpus. Captions were produced by a commercial vision-language model;
external baselines were generated through commercial APIs under their terms of use. No human subjects were involved.
