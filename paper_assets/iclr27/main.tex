\begin{abstract}
Text-to-image models fail at the smallest images people draw on purpose: 12--24\,px RGBA game sprites, where a
handful of colours and hard alpha edges \emph{are} the medium. We show that the usual failure is not a modelling
failure but a target failure. In the standard sprite corpus only 7\% of the images are natively 16\,px or smaller;
the rest is 25--64\,px artwork box-downscaled into the low-resolution buckets, so a model trained on it learns soft,
fifty-colour blobs---and the evaluation reference inherits the same bias, rewarding exactly that. We rebuild the
targets (drop 4{,}888 pixel-identical duplicates that leak 15.5\% of the held-out set into training, forbid
downscaling by more than $1.5\times$, add 14k natively small sprites from three permissively licensed corpora) and we
evaluate against a reference drawn only from real low-resolution pixel art. Under this protocol the data fix alone
takes FD-DINOv2 from 209.6 to 117.9 at 16\,px. We then close the remaining gap where it actually is: real sprites use
a median of six opaque colours while every sampling configuration we tested emits more than fifty. Projecting the
predicted $x_0$ onto a per-sprite $k$-colour palette during the late denoising steps---so that the remaining steps
repair the quantisation seams---reaches $31.8\pm0.5$, better than quantising the finished samples (41.6), and
$2.6\times$ better than the strongest external system under the same palette post-process (50.8 versus 134.4 at
$n{=}200$); it also beats the mixed-provenance ``real'' sprites that the downscale-biased protocol treats as ground
truth (77.0). We report an equally instructive negative result: cross-resolution self-guidance and its internalised
one-pass variant, which win by 40\% under the downscale-biased reference, give no gain once the reference is real
pixel art, while independent-condition guidance transfers and composes with palette projection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Game sprites are among the smallest images anyone draws on purpose: an item or a character on a 12--24\,px RGBA canvas,
built from hard edges, a handful of flat colour regions and a transparent background. At these sizes there is no texture
in which to hide a mistake. One wrong pixel changes the silhouette, and one blended colour reads as blur. The obvious
recipe---prompt a large text-to-image model and shrink its output, or run a pixelization method on it---produces
something recognisable but not a sprite: the result keeps the soft shading and the many colours of an illustration
(Figure~\ref{fig:external}).

This paper is about a failure that sits one level below the model. We train a dedicated generator for this medium---a
72.5M-parameter pixel-space UNet that denoises RGBA sprites directly, conditioned on a frozen CLIP text encoder and on a
\emph{resolution bucket} label---and we find that its weaknesses are inherited from what it is asked to imitate. In the
sprite corpus that work in this area uses, only 7.1\% of the images are natively 16\,px or smaller; two thirds are
25--64\,px artwork that the pipeline box-downsamples into the low-resolution buckets. A model fitted to those targets
learns exactly what they are: soft, fifty-colour blobs. Worse, the standard evaluation reference is drawn from the same
pool, so it rewards the blur it caused: a sampler tuned on it prefers heavy guidance, and the ranking of methods under
that reference does not survive contact with real pixel art.

We therefore change both the targets and the yardstick. On the data side we (i) remove 4{,}888 pixel-identical
duplicates, which had left a pixel-identical twin in training for 15.5\% of the held-out evaluation sprites, (ii) forbid
feeding a sprite to a bucket more than $1.5\times$ below its native size, and (iii) add 14{,}999 natively small sprites
cut from three permissively licensed OpenGameArt collections and captioned with a vision--language model. On the
evaluation side we define the \emph{native protocol}: FD-DINOv2 against real sprites that are natively at most $R$\,px,
never downscaled. At 16\,px the data work alone moves the model from 209.6 to 117.9 under this protocol.

What remains is a property of the medium that continuous diffusion has no way to express. Real 16\,px sprites use a
median of six opaque colours; every sampling configuration we tested---classifier-free guidance at any weight,
cross-resolution self-guidance, independent-condition guidance---emits more than fifty, spending its extra colours on
$\pm$few-level dither inside regions that should be flat. We close that gap inside the sampler: during the late
denoising steps we project the predicted $x_0$ onto a per-sprite $k$-colour palette (batched $k$-means over the opaque
pixels, alpha untouched) and continue denoising, so the remaining steps repair the seams that hard quantisation leaves.
At 16\,px this takes FD from 87.2 to $31.8\pm0.5$; quantising the finished samples instead only reaches 41.6.

Finally, we report a negative result that we consider as informative as the positive one. Cross-resolution
self-guidance---querying the same network under a lower bucket label and extrapolating away from that prediction---and
its internalised one-pass variant were, on the downscale-biased reference, our strongest method (a 40\% improvement over
the best-tuned CFG). Under the native protocol they give nothing (114.9 and 117.0 versus 113.2 for unguided sampling),
and the internalised variant is actively harmful (170.3). Independent-condition guidance, an external method, transfers
and composes with palette projection. Which reference set one scores against decides which of these conclusions one
publishes.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item A diagnosis of the targets used for tiny-sprite generation: native-resolution statistics, duplicate leakage, and
  the resulting bias of the standard evaluation reference; and a rebuilt corpus plus the \emph{native protocol} that
  removes both (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item \textbf{Palette-projected sampling}: a projection of the predicted $x_0$ onto a per-sprite $k$-colour palette in
  the late denoising steps, which is where the remaining distance to real pixel art actually lies. It lowers FD-DINOv2
  at 16\,px from 87.2 to $31.8\pm0.5$ (3 seeds), beats post-hoc quantisation (41.6), and costs 1.3 points of CLIP
  retrieval at $k{=}6$ (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item An external comparison in which every system receives the same palette post-process: at 16/20/24\,px we reach
  50.8/152.5/192.2 against 134.4/448.6/478.0 for gpt-image-2 with downscaling, 150.1/473.9/472.4 for FLUX.2-klein with a
  pixel-art LoRA, and 244.2/526.3/527.2 for SDXL with Pixel Art XL (Section~\ref{sec:external}).
  \item A negative result with its mechanism: cross-resolution guidance and its internalisation win by 40\% under a
  downscale-biased reference and vanish under a native one; we give the per-image statistics (palette size, flat-region
  fraction, contrast) that explain both outcomes (Section~\ref{sec:why}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Guidance references.}
CFG \citep{HoSalimans2022} extrapolates the conditional prediction away from an unconditional one. Many variants keep
that reference and reshape the update: guidance intervals \citep{Kynkaanniemi2024}, APG \citep{Sadat2024apg}, frequency
decoupling \citep{Sadat2025fdg}, CADS \citep{Sadat2024cads}, CFG++ \citep{Chung2024} and feedback or stage-wise schedules
\citep{Koulischer2025,TVCFG2025}. A second line replaces the reference. Autoguidance \citep{Karras2024} uses a smaller or
less-trained version of the same model. Training-free weak predictions come from perturbed self-attention
\citep[PAG, SAG, SEG;][]{Ahn2024,Hong2023,Hong2024}, sliding windows \citep{SWG2024}, stochastic sub-networks
\citep{S2Guidance2025}, a random condition or a perturbed time embedding \citep[ICG, TSG;][]{Sadat2024icg}, or a
semantically degraded text condition \citep[CDG;][]{CDG2026}. Other weak predictions come from auxiliary or internal
heads \citep{IG2025,SGGBR2026,SSG2026}. CDG is closest in spirit: it too degrades one condition of the same network.
It degrades the \emph{text}, whereas we degrade the \emph{resolution label} and keep the caption fixed. We run CDG, ICG,
TSG, SEG and PAG under our protocol (Table~\ref{tab:guidance}).

\paragraph{Guidance without a second pass.}
Guidance distillation \citep{Meng2023distill} regresses a student onto a guided teacher. Model guidance
\citep{Tang2025mg} and guidance-free training \citep{Chen2025gft} instead change the training objective so that its fixed
point is the guided prediction. GFT does this with a stop-gradient unconditional reference, a scalar $\beta$ input, and
the true noise as the target, and reports parity with CFG. We use GFT's objective unchanged and swap its reference.
Our $\beta$ input reuses the guidance-scale embedding of latent consistency models \citep{Luo2023lcm}.

\paragraph{Resolution conditioning.}
Multi-resolution training with a size or bucket label is standard: aspect-ratio bucketing \citep{NovelAI2022}, SDXL's
micro-conditioning \citep{Podell2023}, Matryoshka diffusion \citep{Gu2023}, FiT \citep{Lu2024} and simple diffusion
\citep{Hoogeboom2023}. SDXL documents that a small \texttt{original\_size} reproduces the blur of low-resolution training
images, and common practice passes it to the negative branch of CFG \citep{diffusers}. We test this as a reference in
Section~\ref{sec:sdxl} and find no benefit. To our knowledge, no prior work uses a model's own lower-resolution label as a
guidance reference or internalizes one.

\paragraph{Pixel art.}
Most pixel-art work converts an existing image, by optimisation \citep{Gerstner2012}, contrast-aware downscaling
\citep[PixelOE;][]{PixelOE2024} or learned pixelization \citep{Han2018,Wu2022}. Generative approaches include GANs for
character sprites \citep{Coutinho2022}, pose-conditioned sprite sheets \citep{SpriteSheetDiffusion2024}, a
palette-conditioned small DDPM \citep{PixDiffPIG}, SD-$\pi$XL, which optimises one image by score distillation from SDXL
\citep{Binninger2024}, and community LoRAs such as Pixel Art XL \citep{PixelArtXL}. We compare against conversion
pipelines applied to strong generators; SD-$\pi$XL costs about 8 GPU-hours per sprite (Section~\ref{sec:external}). For
evaluation we follow \citet{Stein2023} and use DINOv2 features \citep{Oquab2023} rather than Inception
\citep{Heusel2017,Parmar2022}.

\section{The targets and the protocol}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{What the corpus actually contains.}
Work on tiny sprites uses OpenGameArt \citep{OpenGameArt} together with the Kenney and Liberated Pixel Cup packs. After
cutting sheets into connected components at their native scale and filtering out glyphs and UI, our copy holds 37{,}489
unique sprites. Their native sizes, however, are not tiny: only 710 are at most 12\,px, 2{,}675 at most 16\,px (7.1\%)
and 12{,}516 at most 24\,px, while two thirds are 25--64\,px artwork. A resolution-bucketed model is usually trained by
box-downsampling every sprite into each lower bucket, so 92.9\% of what the 16\,px bucket sees is squashed artwork
rather than pixel art drawn at 16\,px. Only 4.6\% of the corpus is integer-upscaled pixel art that could be recovered by
dividing by its block size, so the softness cannot be undone; it has to be avoided.

\paragraph{Duplicate leakage.}
Hashing every sprite by its exact pixel content reveals 4{,}888 duplicate copies (9.3\% of the corpus). Because
held-out sets are defined by path, a pixel-identical twin of a held-out sprite stays in training: 15.5\% of the 3{,}000
held-out evaluation sprites, and 17.0\% of the 200 used for the external comparison, are memorisable in this way.

\paragraph{Rebuilt targets.}
We (i) drop every duplicate copy and every copy of a held-out sprite, (ii) allow a sprite to feed a bucket only if it is
at most $1.5\times$ larger than that bucket, and (iii) add natively small sprites: three further OpenGameArt licence
collections (CC-BY-4.0, CC-BY-SA-3.0, OGA-BY-3.0) yield 37{,}226 cut sprites, of which 14{,}999 survive the same
filters, near-duplicate collapse of animation frames and a cap of 60 sprites per source sheet. We caption them with
gemini-3.8-flash on numbered image grids, keeping 13{,}987 captions (99.6\% distinct, median 13 words); the provenance
and licence of every sprite is recorded. The rebuilt corpus has 86{,}290 training rows and twice as many natively
$\le 16$\,px sprites as before. Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the per-step counts and the verification sheets.

\paragraph{The native protocol.}
FD-DINOv2 \citep{Stein2023} is computed against a reference set of real sprites pushed through the model's own
preprocessing. The usual choice---a random sample of the corpus---inherits the bias above. We therefore report the
\emph{native protocol}: the reference at resolution $R$ is drawn only from sprites whose native size is at most $R$, so
no downscaling ever touches it (1{,}337 reference and 1{,}338 held-out images at 16\,px, real-vs-real floor 4.47). We
report the biased protocol as well, because the two disagree systematically (Section~\ref{sec:why}), and we mark which
is which in every table.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{A resolution-bucketed pixel-space sprite model}
\label{sec:model}

We generate RGBA sprites on an $R \times R$ canvas, $R \in \{12, 16, 20, 24\}$, from a caption $c$. The denoiser
$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c, b)$ is a UNet (diffusers \texttt{UNet2DConditionModel}; 72.5M parameters; channel widths
128/256/512; two cross-attention stages at $R$ and $R/2$; no attention at $R/4$ except the mid block). It operates
directly on the 4-channel image. Text enters through cross-attention to the 77 token states of a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32
text encoder \citep{Radford2021}. The resolution bucket $b \in \mathcal{B} = \{12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 48, 64\}$ enters as a
class embedding added to the timestep embedding, and it is never dropped. We train with $\epsilon$-prediction
\citep{Ho2020}, a cosine schedule with $T = 1000$, AdamW at a constant learning rate of $5\cdot10^{-5}$, EMA 0.999 and
10\% caption dropout. We sample with 100 ancestral DDPM steps from the EMA weights. The main model, \textbf{v8n}, is
trained from scratch for 80k steps on the rebuilt corpus of Section~\ref{sec:data}; \textbf{v8f2} is the same run
without the new sprites and \textbf{v7r} is the same architecture on the original targets, used as controls.

\subsection{Palette-projected sampling}
\label{sec:palette}

A 16\,px sprite is defined as much by how few colours it uses as by its shape: in the native reference set the median
sprite has six opaque colours (12\,px: 5; 20 and 24\,px: 7) and 22.6\% use at most four. A continuous diffusion model in
RGB has no mechanism that snaps a region to a single value, and none of our samplers does: measured on 600 samples,
plain CFG emits 56.2 colours and the best guidance rule 50.9, with the fraction of adjacent opaque pixel pairs that are
exactly equal at 0.36--0.38 against 0.46 for real sprites. The extra colours are dither of a few levels inside regions
that the model has, in effect, already decided are flat.

We remove that dither inside the sampler. At a step with $t/T \le f$ we form the current estimate
$\hat x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\tilde e)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, cluster its \emph{opaque} RGB pixels into $k$
groups with a batched $k$-means (8 iterations, deterministic initialisation at evenly spaced luminance quantiles),
replace every pixel by its centroid, leave transparent pixels and the alpha channel untouched, and map the result back
to a noise prediction,
\begin{equation}
  \hat x_0^{\text{pal}} = \Pi_k(\hat x_0), \qquad
  \tilde e \;\leftarrow\; \bigl(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat x_0^{\text{pal}}\bigr) / \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t},
  \label{eq:palette}
\end{equation}
before taking the scheduler step. The projection is thus a hard constraint applied to the model's own belief, not a
post-process: the steps that follow see the quantised state and repair the seams it creates, which is why doing this
inside the sampler beats quantising the finished image (31.8 vs.\ 41.6 at 16\,px, Section~\ref{sec:main16}). It adds no
network evaluations; $k$-means on $R^2 \le 576$ pixels is negligible next to a UNet pass. We use $f = 0.5$ throughout and
report $k$ per resolution; $k$ can also be drawn per sprite from the corpus histogram, which we ablate.

\subsection{Guidance rules we compare}
\label{sec:guidance}

All guidance rules combine the target-resolution prediction $e_{\text{strong}} = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c, b_R)$ with a
reference $\eref$ on the same state, $\tilde e = \eref + w\,(e_{\text{strong}} - \eref)$. CFG \citep{HoSalimans2022} uses
the empty caption. ICG \citep{Sadat2024icg} replaces the caption embedding by i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise with the
embedding's standard deviation, redrawn at every step, which needs no unconditional training and, as we show, is the
rule that survives the change of protocol. A bucketed model also offers a reference with no extra weights---the same
network under a \emph{lower} bucket label, optionally through an early EMA snapshot (the \emph{composed} variant)---and
that reference can be internalised into the weights with guidance-free training \citep{Chen2025gft} so that one forward
pass reproduces the two-pass rule. These cross-resolution variants are the ones that win under the biased protocol and
fail under the native one; Appendix~\ref{app:crossres} gives their equations and their full tables.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

For every configuration we draw one sample for each of 3{,}000 held-out captions, the recaptions of 3{,}000 held-out
sprites, so the prompt distribution matches the data. Our metric is the Fréchet distance of DINOv2-small CLS features
\citep{Oquab2023,Stein2023} (FD; lower is better), computed at the native resolution $R$: images are composited on white
and nearest-neighbour upsampled to 224\,px. We report it under two reference sets. The \emph{native} reference uses only
sprites that are natively at most $R$\,px (Section~\ref{sec:data}); its real-vs-real floor is 4.47 at 16\,px. The
\emph{biased} reference is the conventional one, a random sample of the corpus, of which 92.9\% is downscaled artwork at
16\,px; its floor is 3.45. Seed-to-seed standard deviation of our headline configuration is 0.49 over three seeds, and
an independent re-training of the same recipe lands within 2.5 FD of the first (20.33/154.30 versus 20.31/151.85 at 20k
steps, biased/native), so we do not interpret differences below that scale. We also report CLIP ViT-B/32 image--text
similarity ($100\cos$) and retrieval R@1 of the own caption among 100.

\paragraph{Ablation: data versus sampler.}
Applying the final sampler to the model trained on the original targets already gives 42.9, so most of the gain is on
the sampling side; the rebuilt targets then remove a further 26\% (42.9 $\to$ 41.6 with the bucket policy alone, and
$\to$ 31.8 once the new sprites are added). The two are complementary: palette projection on top of plain CFG reaches
only 58.4, against 31.8 with ICG, so the guidance and the projection do different work.

\paragraph{Palette size.}
Sweeping $k$ at 16\,px gives 38.1 / 31.8 / 33.7 / 36.9 / 50.6 for $k = 3 / 4 / 5 / 6 / 8$, and 39.9 when $k$ is drawn per
sprite from the corpus histogram. The optimum sits at the median palette size of the real data (six colours, 22.6\% of
real sprites use four or fewer), not below it, which is the sanity check that the projection is matching the medium
rather than exploiting the metric.

\paragraph{The cost in prompt alignment.}
Palette projection trades a little text faithfulness for a large distributional gain: CLIP $100\cos$ / R@1 are
27.60 / 22.3\% for ICG $w{=}2$ alone, 27.38 / 21.0\% at $k{=}6$ and 27.07 / 17.9\% at $k{=}4$ (real sprites: 19.68 /
1.1\%). We therefore report $k{=}4$ as the best-FD setting and $k{=}6$ as the balanced one.

\subsection{Other resolutions}
\label{sec:otherres}

The same recipe transfers, with the palette size tracking the data (median 5 / 6 / 7 / 7 colours at 12 / 16 / 20 /
24\,px). Native FD for v8n with ICG $w{=}2$: 156.5 at 12\,px ($k{=}4$; CFG 1.5 gives 167.3), 96.7 at 20\,px ($k{=}8$;
$k{=}6$ 99.1, $k{=}4$ 110.7, no projection 117.0) and 124.2 at 24\,px ($k{=}6$; $k{=}8$ 130.1, $k{=}4$ 128.4, no
projection 186.8, CFG 1.5 229.2). The gain from projection grows with resolution at 24\,px, where the model has the most
room to dither.

\section{Why a lower resolution is a good reference}
\label{sec:why}

This section asks what the reference must satisfy. Its results constrain the design of Section~\ref{sec:method}; they do
not establish a mechanism.

\paragraph{Direction matters.}
If any wrong bucket acted as a generic unconditional-like reference, a higher or a random bucket would work as well as a
lower one. Neither does. On v7r at 16\,px, a bucket drawn uniformly at random at every step (ICG applied to the
resolution label) gives 12.23 / 12.68 at $w = 1.5 / 2$. That is \emph{worse} than best CFG (11.10, seed 0), whereas the
lower bucket gives 8.81. On v7h, every higher-bucket reference is worse than best CFG at every resolution (16\,px:
buckets 20 / 24 / 64 give 17.14 / 17.27 / 19.34 vs.\ 12.24; Appendix~\ref{app:v7h}). On v7h the label also acts as a
monotone contrast knob: the pure belief's local contrast rises from 21.6 to 31.9 to 40.9 for buckets 12, 24 and 64 at
16\,px. Only its low-contrast end is useful. Isotropic perturbation of the same embedding (TSG) is harmful at every
setting we tried (17.73--37.28; Table~\ref{tab:guidance}).

\paragraph{The guidance is not a denoising-error correction.}
A tempting alternative to Eq.~\eqref{eq:gft} is a learned module that reads the lower-bucket belief and corrects the
strong prediction, trained with the plain denoising loss. Any such module converges to $\mu$ and can extrapolate away from
$r$ only as far as the finite network's residual is linearly visible in $d = e_{\text{strong}} - r$. The best such
extrapolation weight is directly measurable: $w^\star(t) = \mathbb{E}[d \cdot (\epsilon - r)] / \mathbb{E}[d \cdot d]$.
This is unbiased because $\epsilon - \mu$ has zero mean given $x_t$. On v7h, on 3,000 held-out sprites at 10 timesteps (and 3,000 training
sprites at 16\,px), $w^\star$ lies in 0.83--1.07 for the label, snapshot and composed references at 16, 20 and 24\,px. It
is often below 1 on held-out data, and extrapolating to $w = 1.5$ increases the denoising error everywhere (by 1--95\%). On the
model's own trajectories, obtained by noising a real sprite and running the deployed sampler for $k \in \{1, 5, 20\}$
steps, $w^\star$ in $x_0$-space stays at or below 1.07 for $k \ge 5$ at all three resolutions (0.64--0.78 is typical at
$k = 5$; Appendix~\ref{app:wstar}). Guidance with $w \approx 1.5$--2 improves FD while \emph{increasing} per-sample
regression error. It shapes the distribution, as CFG does, rather than correcting individual predictions. Hence a
reparameterization whose fixed point is the guided rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:fixedpoint}) is needed; a learned corrector is not
enough.

\paragraph{The reference must act in output space.}
The cheapest form of 1-\nfe{} extrapolation acts on the label embedding itself:
$E[16] + (w-1)(E[16] - E[12])$, used with a single conditional pass. It fails badly on v7h at 16\,px: 39.03 at $w = 1.5$
and 414.27 at $w = 2$, against 16.39 without any guidance. The lower label is useful only as a second prediction that the
update extrapolates away from, and the internalization of Section~\ref{sec:internal} keeps it in that role.

\paragraph{A reference must degrade along an axis the model uses.}
Recaptioning reverses the ranking of the text-axis and resolution-axis references. With BLIP captions (v7h), which the
model largely ignores, ICG is weak (best 8.88 at $w = 1.25$; 11.29 at $w = 1.5$), and the cross-resolution rules are
strong (label 8.52, composed 7.53, best CFG 12.49). With informative captions (v7r), ICG becomes the strongest
training-free reference (7.46), and the cross-resolution rules still help but less (8.86 / 8.50 vs.\ best CFG 11.28).
A random text condition is a weak reference only when the prediction depends on the text. The resolution label
matters regardless of caption quality, since every sprite is trained at several buckets. The two axes are complementary
at 16\,px (Section~\ref{sec:main16}), but only in the internalized form. Placing ICG's condition and the lower bucket in
the same training-free weak pass gives 7.16, no better than ICG alone (7.14, seed 0), and adding the snapshot to that pass
is harmful (11.65).

\paragraph{Where it does not transfer: SDXL's size conditioning.}
\label{sec:sdxl}
SDXL's \texttt{original\_size} micro-conditioning is the closest public analogue of a resolution label. On 1,000
COCO-30k captions (Appendix~\ref{app:sdxl}), adding $1\times(e_{c} - e_{c,\text{orig}=512})$ to CFG 5 leaves FD unchanged
(644.13 vs.\ 644.18). With $\text{orig} = 256$ it is worse (658.98), and without CFG the size reference collapses
(969.68). The only agreement with our model is directional: keeping the caption in the weak branch beats the common
\texttt{negative\_original\_size} practice (650.98). We believe that SDXL's size condition encodes the blur of upsampled
low-resolution training images rather than a coarser target grid, so it does not yield a structure-aligned,
lower-contrast belief of the same image. The reference is a property of models trained natively over a ladder of grids.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) \emph{Scope.} Everything is measured on models we trained, from one architecture family, on one corpus of game
sprites. We have not tested a second domain, and SDXL's size conditioning does not transfer. (ii) \emph{Metric.} FD with
DINOv2 on images of 12--24\,px, upsampled to 224\,px, assigns one sprite pixel to one or a few patches. It is sensitive to
exactly the colour and contrast statistics that guidance changes. We report CLIP alignment for most 16\,px rows, but we
have no human study. The CLIP evaluator also shares its text tower with our model's frozen encoder. (iii) \emph{Tuning.}
Guidance weights, the ICG combination, the reference rung and the 10k-step fine-tuning length were all selected on the
same held-out FD that we report; there is no separate validation split. Seed and re-training variation (Section~\ref{sec:protocol})
is of the same order as some of the smaller differences, which we do not interpret. (iv) \emph{Coverage.} 12\,px needs an
extra 8\,px rung, and its internalized result is pending. Several 20/24\,px rows are single-seed. The combination with ICG
helps at 16\,px but not at 20\,px. (v) \emph{Novelty of the objective.} The training objective is GFT's; our claim is the
reference and the evidence that it, not the objective, carries the gain.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
A diffusion model trained over a ladder of resolutions contains its own best weak model: the same network under a lower
resolution label. Extrapolating away from it is directional, acts in output space and is not an error correction. Folding
it into the weights with a guidance-free-training objective gives a one-pass sampler that matches or beats every
training-free guidance rule we tested at 16\,px and is the best single method at 20 and 24\,px. Combined with ICG it reaches 6.25 at 16\,px, against 11.28 for best CFG and 82--187 for
large-model-plus-conversion pipelines on the shared protocol.

\section*{Ethics statement}
The model generates small game sprites. It adds no capability beyond existing text-to-image systems, and its outputs
(12--24\,px) have little potential for targeted misuse. Its intended application raises the usual concerns of generative
art tools, namely the displacement of pixel artists and the reproduction of styles present in the training corpus. The
training data are community sprites from OpenGameArt and the Kenney and Liberated Pixel Cup packs. OpenGameArt
submissions carry per-asset licences (CC0, CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, GPL, OGA-BY) that were not recorded at collection time, so a
per-asset licence audit precedes any release of the corpus. Captions were produced by a commercial vision-language model;
external baselines were generated through commercial APIs under their terms of use. No human subjects were involved.
